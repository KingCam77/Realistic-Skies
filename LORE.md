# Sea Legends and Constellations
*The sky of Realistic Skies, as sailors know it*

> Every sailor learns the stars twice: once from the Almanac, and once from the old hands on the night watch. This is the second lesson.

Turn on **Star Labels** in the config and look at a star at night: its name, atlas designation and constellation appear next to it, and the constellation's figure is drawn on the sky. The **Star Chart** sold in the capitals shows the most useful figures, and the full atlas chart with every constellation is in `docs/star-chart-constellations.jpg`.

## Reading a designation
Stars without a name go by their atlas designation, for example **2Y-061-165**:

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **2** | part of the sky: 1 = 21h to 3h, 2 = 3h to 9h, 3 = 9h to 15h, 4 = 15h to 21h |
| **Y** | band of declination: A +60..+90, C +30..+60, B 0..+30, Y 0..-30, X -30..-60, Z -60..-90 |
| **061** | right ascension in tenths of an hour (6.1h) |
| **165** | declination in tenths of a degree (16.5, south because of the Y) |

Single letters (A, B, a, b...) are the bright stars of the Almanac.

## Around the North Star

### Corvin's Sloop
Low in the north, close to the North Star, a little boat of stars sails around the pole all night, every night: north of about 30 degrees it never sets. Its brightest star is **Corvin**, the captain himself, and at its stern burns the **Lantern**. Ahead of its bow flashes **Sickle**, the sickle fin of the sea monster; the beast's gaping jaws, **Morvath's Maw**, with **Morvath** herself, lie beyond it, always turned away. Behind the Sloop's stern and a little to the side shines **Willow**. Close by, the **Northern Bow** curves from the North Star through Ilios down to Roxe.

### Morvath's Maw
Beyond the Sloop's bow gape the jaws of the sea monster: a wide loop of stars with **Sickle**, her fin, at one end and bright **Morvath** herself at the other. Her maw stays turned away from the Sloop that chases her night after night.

### Widow's Grief
Willow herself, on her knees at the Sloop's stern. Her shins rest level with the stern, her body rises from her knees, and her head is the bright star **Willow**; from her shoulders her arms are raised away from the pole in supplication. On a chart with north at the top she hangs upside down; she kneels upright when she stands high above the North Star. Find Willow and you have found her.

### The Northern Bow
From the North Star a gentle bow of stars curves through **Ilios** down to **Roxe**. Find the arc of the Bow and follow it up towards its tip: the last bright star before the faint tip is the North Star.

### The Arrowhead
In the northern sky, some 30 degrees from the North Star, lies a stone arrowhead; the bright star **Flint** sits on one edge, next to the tip. Its notched base always faces the North Star: draw a line from the tip through the notch and carry it on about three times the arrowhead's length, past Ilios, and you arrive at the North Star.

## Around Oriens

### The Harpoon
A whaling harpoon lies on the equator. Its point is **Spearhead**, and from the middle of its shaft two long barbs sweep back: one ends at bright Oriens, the other runs through **Lashing** down to **Barb**. It is Captain Corvin's harpoon, see his legend.

### Oriens to Milnead
Oriens is the most noticeable star on the equator, rising due east, at the tip of one of the **Harpoon**'s barbs. Draw a line from Oriens across to Spearhead, the harpoon's point, turn and follow the long line to **Mouser**, the brightest yellow star around. Milnead rises just below Mouser, about an hour after Spearhead clears the horizon.

### Ship's Cat
Every ship had its cat, and so does the sky: **Mouser** is her head, two little paws reach out beside it, and her body runs down to Milnead, where her hind legs part. In the east she rises head first, as if peeking over the rail. Follow her down from Mouser and you have found Milnead.

### The Mouse
Some twenty degrees north of the Ship's Cat a little triangle of stars, bright **Nibble** at its head, scurries away from her, its long tail still trailing back towards the cat.

### The Gull
On the equator, some seventeen degrees west of Oriens (so it rises a little over an hour before it), a gull spreads its wings: seven stars with **Feather** the brightest, one wing reaching north, the other west with a small hook at its tip, and a short tail to the south. Sailors say she is the one who warned the Fisherman, and stole his pipe.

### The Pipe
Some twenty degrees west of the Gull a sailor's pipe lies across the equator: a small four-sided bowl of stars just south of it, and a long straight stem running north from the bowl to the mouthpiece, **Stem**. It belongs to the Fisherman; see his legend for how it got here.

### The Whirlpool
About twenty-five degrees north of Oriens a crooked chain of six stars, among them **Eddy**, twists through the sky like water spinning down into the deep.

### The Fisherman and his Net
Just north of the Whirlpool a fisherman, his brightest star **Grumble**, stands with his arms spread, and beside him hangs his net, a loose four-sided loop of stars around **Cork**, cast over the Whirlpool below. Why he stands like that, see the legend of the Fisherman and the Gull.

### The Lug Sail
Just south of the equator a four-cornered sail stands on the sea. The bright star **Peak** is its top corner, where the yard ends high up; the yard runs down from it to the throat, the luff steps down from there to the lower front corner, **Tack**, the foot runs along the bottom to the clew, and the leech climbs back up to Peak.

## The other half of the sky

### The Scorpion
North of the equator, with the bright star **Dhanab** in its curling tail: the tail ends in an upturned sting, and at the other end two claws open wide.

### The Hunting Horn
Lying right across the equator, a curved horn of eight stars with the bright stars **Buq** and **Sada** at its wide bell; its narrow mouthpiece points south.

### The Witch's Hat
A crooked pointed hat of six stars just north of the equator, with the bright star **Witchlight** at its crown. How it got there, see the legend of the Witch's Hat.

### The Harp
The frame of a great harp, a tall triangle outlined by three bright stars, **Naghma** at the top, **Watar** low on the equator and a third between them, with one fainter star on the pillar between that third star and Watar. The strings you have to imagine.

### The Haymaker
South of the equator a mower stands with his legs apart and his head raised. His great scythe runs right across the figure, from the bright star **Snath** at the end of the handle, through his hands, to **Heel**, where the curved blade begins. He walks right along the Milky Way, with his Bull following behind; see their legend.

## The far south

### The Bull
Far down in the south a bull lowers his head, as if to charge (though see the legend of the Haymaker and the Bull). Two long horns point the same way, one of them ending at **Qarn**; his broad brow runs across the top of the figure, and his face narrows down to the muzzle. The bright star **Ain**, alone inside the figure, is his eye. From the northern islands only his horns and brow clear the horizon; sail south to see the whole beast.

### The Cobra
Close to the southern pole a cobra rears up to strike. Its tail starts at the bright star **Oboshi** and wriggles up, its body rises tall and arches over, and its head hangs forward with **Kashira** at the back of the skull, ready to strike. Only seen from the far southern seas; how she got there, see the legend of the Cobra.

### The Fiddler Crab
In the southern sky, not far from the Cobra, a crab with a square shell and two jointed legs raised behind it. One claw is huge, a wide-open pincer tipped by the bright star **Mikhlab**; in the small claw it holds a pearl, brilliant **Lulu**. Sailors say he stole it from the sea witch's treasure and has been backing away across the sky with it ever since.

### The Southern Pole
No star marks the southern pole: the nearest is a faint star of the Cobra, eight degrees away. But the Cobra points to it. From bright Oboshi at the tip of its tail, draw a line through the next bright star of the tail and carry it on three times as far: it runs straight into the pole. As in the north, the height of the pole above the horizon is your latitude, here south.

## Legends

### The Legend of Corvin's Sloop
Captain Corvin's brother was taken by a sea monster, Morvath, and Corvin swore to kill it. His wife Willow begged him to stay, but he sailed. The cunning beast always slipped away just ahead of his bow; in all those years the only part of her Corvin ever saw was the sickle of her fin cutting the waves. He chased it across every sea until the ocean finally took him and his sloop.

But the sea did not keep them. Sloop, captain and quarry were set among the stars, and there the chase goes on: the Sloop circles the North Star night after night with Sickle, Morvath's fin, always just ahead and never a mile closer, while her maw stays turned away. The harpoon Corvin never got to throw slipped from his hand as the sea closed over the Sloop and fell far to the south, onto the equator: the Harpoon, with bright Oriens at the tip of one of its barbs, rising due east every night, its point and barb still aimed at a monster that is never there. Behind the Sloop Willow kneels where the stern passed, her arms raised in a prayer that was never answered; sailors call her Widow's Grief. Once each night, in the south of the islands, she sinks below the sea to hide her face. Far out in the dark drifts the faint planet sailors call Teardrop: the one tear she let fall into the sky.

### The Legend of the Witch's Hat
A sea witch once drove storm after storm against the coast, sinking every boat that put out. One night a hunter climbed the cliffs with his great horn and blew it into the teeth of the gale. The blast was so loud that the wind itself turned, tore the pointed hat from the witch's head and flung it into the sky, where it still hangs just north of the equator. The horn slipped from the hunter's hands and fell after it: the Hunting Horn lies across the equator, between the hat and the Scorpion, the witch's pet, which she set in the sky to guard her hat. Sailors say she will come back for it one stormy night, and that a ship which hears a horn in the wind should make for harbour.

### The Ship's Cat and the Mouse
On a long voyage, when the stores were nearly gone, a mouse stole the very last ship's biscuit from the galley. The ship's cat saw her and gave chase: up the mast, along the yard and up the rigging, until the mouse ran right off the masthead into the sky with the cat leaping after her. The chase never ended. Every night the cat climbs the sky with her paws stretched out, and every night the Mouse stays some twenty degrees ahead, her long tail trailing back just out of reach. Sailors say that while the cat is in the sky no mouse will touch the ship's biscuit.

### The Fisherman and the Gull
A sailor once stopped for the night in a little fishing village and told of a place out at sea where the fish were past counting. One fisherman made up his mind to be the first to cast his net there. No sooner said than done: he raised his lug sail and set course for the shoal, but the current caught his boat and carried it off. A gull was circling over the boat, and she saw that he was about to cast his net right over a whirlpool. She cried and cried to warn him, but the fisherman, hungry for his catch, would not listen to a bird, and cast his net straight into the whirlpool.

He barely managed to haul his poor net back, empty and torn, and in his vexation he turned on the gull and cursed her with every word he knew. The gull would not put up with such insults: while he was not looking, she snatched his pipe and flew off with it.

They are all in the sky now, in a row along the equator. His little Lug Sail still stands on the sea, the Fisherman stands with his arms spread wide, still shouting after the bird, his Net hangs empty over the Whirlpool, and his Pipe drifts across the sky right beside the Gull, who has never given it back. Sailors say: when a gull cries over your boat, listen.

### The Haymaker and the Bull
This is the tale old sailors tell the ship's boys on a quiet watch. The Haymaker was a farmer who never went to sea, and the Bull was his own, the strongest beast in the valley and as gentle as a lamb. For forty summers they worked the same meadow together, and when the old man died the bull would not leave the field, so the sky took them both. Now, night after night, they cross the starry meadow side by side. The Haymaker goes first, walking right along the Milky Way and swinging his great scythe through the stars; that pale band across the sky is the swath of mown hay he leaves behind him, which is why some sailors call it the Straw Road. The Bull follows close behind with his head lowered. Sailors who see him for the first time on the southern runs think he is about to charge, but he is only grazing on the starry grass his master has cut. Old hands say that while the mower is at work there will be no storm that night.

### The Legend of the Cobra
In the Emerald Archipelago they tell of Rinsai the Wanderer, who walked the land and sailed the sea for longer than anyone can remember and helped everyone he met on his way. One season he came to a village where people and cattle were dying in their sleep, and on every body there were only two small marks: the bite of a single snake. The villagers whispered that a great and cunning cobra was killing everyone who had dared to settle on her land.

Rinsai went into the jungle alone. For thirty days and thirty nights he tracked her through the green dark, and on the thirty-first night he found her. The cobra fought with all her fury, and twice her fangs came within a finger's breadth of his throat; but at last Rinsai seized her by the neck and hurled her upward with such strength that she struck the vault of heaven and stuck fast there.

They say Rinsai still wanders the world, helping whoever needs it. And the Cobra still circles the southern sky, reared up and ready to strike, for she knows that the gods raise such heroes to the heavens in the end, and she means to be waiting for him. Southern sailors owe her one kindness all the same: the tip of her tail points to the still centre of the southern sky.
